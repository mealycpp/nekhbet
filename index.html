Airflow is an actuator-disk momentum model: each prop's induced velocity comes from its thrust, the wake contracts and speeds up to twice the disk velocity, swirls with the prop, bends with the wind, and spreads along the ground as a wall jet. The tracer animation runs in slow motion so the wake stays readable; the colors show the true air speed. It is physics-based but it is not a Navier–Stokes CFD solution. Ground effect uses the Cheeseman–Bennett relation.

Simplified model: 1.9 kg all-up, 4 × 1.6 kgf max thrust, drag area about 0.035 m², sea-level air for every gravity preset, fixed-gain attitude and position loops. Use it to compare settings, not to clear a flight.

How one arm is built

  1. A 16 mm square carbon tube is bolted between the frame plates and never moves.
  2. A 12 mm square carbon tube slides inside it on two bushings: one fixed at the outer tube's mouth, one riding on the inner tube's rear end. Square-in-square stops the motor's reaction torque from rotating the arm.
  3. The L16-R hangs under the fixed tube on a printed rear clevis bracket and a printed front clamp that wraps both tube and actuator.
  4. The actuator rod pins to a printed collar clamped on the exposed part of the inner tube, just past the outer tube's mouth. This replaces the slot I drew earlier: the outer tube stays uncut, so it keeps its full bending stiffness.
  5. The printed motor mount (socket sleeve plus pad with heat-set inserts) is bonded and through-bolted on the inner tube's tip.
  6. At full extension 60 mm of inner tube is still inside the outer tube, about 5× the inner tube width.
